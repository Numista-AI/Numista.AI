## 1. Rank 4: Early Federal Type Thin Spots

### Minor Coppers & Obsolete Denominations (E1–E7)
1. **Half Cents (1793–1857):**
   - **Liberty Cap (1793–1797):** Left-facing bust (1793, pole to cap); Right-facing bust (1794–1797). Engraved by Joseph Wright and Robert Scot.
   - **Draped Bust (1800–1808):** Scot design. Obverse draped bust; reverse wreath enclosing HALF CENT, 1/200 fraction below.
   - **Classic Head (1809–1836):** John Reich design. Fillet inscribed LIBERTY in hair; no fraction on reverse.
   - **Braided Hair (1840–1857):** Christian Gobrecht design. Proof-only 1840–1848; circulation strikes 1849–1857.
2. **Large Cents (1793–1857):**
   - **Chain Cent (1793):** Obverse Flowing Hair, LIBERTY above, date below; reverse 15 interlocking chain links enclosing ONE CENT and 1/100. (Critical parity check: 15-link chain reverse).
   - **Wreath Cent (1793):** Laurel wreath reverse with trefoil/sprig above date. Vine and bars or lettered edge.
   - **Liberty Cap Cent (1793–1796):** Scot/Wright design; head right with cap on pole behind.
   - **Draped Bust Cent (1796–1807):** Draped bust right; fractional value 1/100 on reverse.
   - **Classic Head Cent (1808–1814):** Reich design; softer copper composition.
   - **Coronet / Matron Head Cent (1816–1839):** Gobrecht/Scot modifications.
   - **Braided Hair Cent (1839–1857):** Gobrecht design; Petite Head vs. Mature Head varieties.
3. **Two-Cent Pieces (1864–1873):**
   - Authorized by Act of April 22, 1864 (James Longacre design).
   - **Historical Milestone:** First US coin to bear the national motto IN GOD WE TRUST on the obverse ribbon above the heraldic shield.
   - **Key Varieties:** 1864 Small Motto vs. Large Motto.
4. **Three-Cent Silver / Trimes (1851–1873):**
   - Act of March 3, 1851 (designed to facilitate postage stamp purchases after rate cut to 3¢). Smallest US silver coin (0.75 to 0.80 grams).
   - **Type 1 (1851–1853):** 75% silver, 25% copper; single border star.
   - **Type 2 (1854–1858):** 90% silver, 10% copper; two outlines around star, olive sprig and arrows in reverse C.
   - **Type 3 (1859–1873):** Single outline around star.
5. **Three-Cent Nickel (1865–1889):**
   - Cupro-nickel (75% Cu, 25% Ni). Longacre design with Liberty head coronet; Roman numeral III within laurel wreath.
6. **Twenty-Cent Pieces (1875–1878):**
   - William Barber design; shortest-lived regular US coin denomination. Plain edge, Seated Liberty obverse, raised eagle on reverse. Often confused in commerce with Seated quarter.
7. **Half Dimes (1794–1873):**
   - Flowing Hair (1794–1795), Draped Bust Small Eagle (1796–1797), Draped Bust Heraldic Eagle (1800–1805), Capped Bust (1829–1837), Seated Liberty (1837–1873: No Stars, Stars, Arrows, Legend on Obverse).

### Early Silver Dollars & Seated Series (S1–S3)
1. **Flowing Hair & Draped Bust Dollars (1794–1804):**
   - 1794 (estimated ~1,758 mintage, Robert Scot dies).
   - Draped Bust Small Eagle (1795–1798) vs. Heraldic Eagle (1798–1804). The famous 1804 Dollar was struck in the 1830s and 1850s for diplomatic gift presentation sets and collectors (Class I, II, III restrikes/fantasies).
2. **Gobrecht Dollars (1836–1839):**
   - Christian Gobrecht design with Titian Peale flying eagle reverse. Die alignments: Coin turn vs. Medal turn; Eagle flying in starry field vs. plain field.
3. **Seated Liberty Silver (1837–1891):**
   - Sub-types: No Stars (1837–1838), With Stars (1838–1853), Arrows & Rays (1853), Arrows at Date (1854–1855), With Motto (1866–1891), Arrows at Date (1873–1874 Trade-weight transition).
4. **Trade Dollars (1873–1885):**
   - Authorized to compete with Mexican 8 Reales in Asian commerce. 420 grains, 0.900 fine silver. Circulation issues (1873–1878); Proof-only issues (1879–1885).

### Pre-1933 United States Gold Coinage (G1–G7)
1. **Gold Dollars (1849–1889):**
   - Type 1 (1849–1854): Liberty Head, 13 mm.
   - Type 2 (1854–1856): Indian Princess / Small Head, 15 mm.
   - Type 3 (1856–1889): Indian Princess / Large Head, 15 mm.
2. **Quarter Eagles (.50, 1796–1929):**
   - Capped Bust to Right (1796–1807), Capped Draped Bust (1808), Capped Head (1821–1834), Classic Head (1834–1839), Liberty Head (1840–1907), Bela Lyon Pratt Incuse Indian Head (1908–1929).
3. **Three-Dollar Gold Pieces (1854–1889):**
   - Longacre Indian Princess design, tobacco/corn/wheat/cotton agricultural wreath reverse enclosing 3 DOLLARS.
4. **Half Eagles (.00, 1795–1929):**
   - First gold coin struck by US Mint (July 31, 1795). Types: Capped Bust to Right (1795–1807), Capped Bust to Left (1807–1812), Capped Head (1813–1834), Classic Head (1834–1838), Liberty Head (1839–1908: No Motto / With Motto), Incuse Indian Head (1908–1929).
5. **Eagles (.00, 1795–1933):**
   - Capped Bust to Right (1795–1804: Small Eagle / Heraldic Eagle), Liberty Head (1838–1907), Augustus Saint-Gaudens Indian Head with feather headdress (1907–1933: Wire Rim, Rolled Rim, No Motto, With Motto).
6. **Double Eagles (.00, 1849–1933):**
   - James B. Longacre Coronet Liberty (1849–1907): Type 1 No Motto, Value TWENTY D. (1849–1866); Type 2 With Motto, Value TWENTY D. (1866–1876); Type 3 With Motto, Value spelled TWENTY DOLLARS (1877–1907).
   - Augustus Saint-Gaudens Standing Liberty (1907–1933): 1907 Ultra High Relief, 1907 High Relief (Roman numerals MCMVII), 1907–1908 No Motto, 1908–1933 With Motto.

### M8a: US-Philippine Territorial Coinage (1903–1935)
- **Authority:** Act of Congress of March 2, 1903. Legal tender for the Philippine Islands under US governance.
- **Mints:** Philadelphia (no mint mark), San Francisco (S), Manila (M — only US branch mint established outside the continental US, opened 1920).
- **Designers:** Melicio Figueroa (native Filipino sculptor; sculpted obverse models), Charles E. Barber (adapted reverse US shield models).
- **Denominations & Specifications:**
  - **Half Centavo (1903–1908):** Bronze. Seated man holding hammer on anvil, smoking Mt. Mayon in background; legend FILIPINAS. Reverse heraldic American eagle over Philippine shield, legend UNITED STATES OF AMERICA and date.
  - **One Centavo (1903–1936):** Bronze. Seated man at anvil / Mt. Mayon.
  - **Five Centavos (1903–1935):** Copper-nickel. Seated man at anvil.
  - **Ten Centavos (1903–1935):** Silver (Reduced size/weight in 1907 due to rising silver bullion prices). Standing female allegory holding hammer, anvil, Mt. Mayon in background.
  - **Twenty Centavos (1903–1935):** Silver (0.750 fine). Standing female allegory.
  - **Fifty Centavos (1903–1935):** Silver (0.750 fine). Standing female allegory.
  - **One Peso (1903–1912):** Large silver dollar size. 1903–1906 (0.900 fine, 26.95 g); reduced in 1907–1912 (0.800 fine, 20.0 g) to prevent silver hoarding.

### M8b: Commonwealth of the Philippines Coinage (1935–1945)
- **Constitutional Milestone:** Established by the Tydings–McDuffie Act of 1934; Philippine Commonwealth established Nov 15, 1935.
- **Reverse Modification:** Reverse legend changed to COMMONWEALTH OF THE PHILIPPINES with new Commonwealth coat of arms (eagle perched on modified crest).
- **Key Issues:**
  - 1936 Commemorative Issues: Murphy-Quezon, Roosevelt-Quezon One Peso and Fifty Centavos.
  - 1944–1945 US Wartime Coinage: Struck at Philadelphia, San Francisco, and Denver (D) to support liberation forces. Include Fifty, Twenty, Ten, Five, and One Centavo with COMMONWEALTH reverse.

### M8c: US-Philippine Paper Currency Types (1903–1945)
1. **Silver Certificates (1903–1918):**
   - Issued by the Insular Government of the Philippine Islands. Printed by the US Bureau of Engraving and Printing (BEP) in Washington, D.C.
   - Denominations: 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500 Pesos. Payable to the bearer on demand in silver pesos or US gold coin.
2. **Treasury Certificates (1918–1941):**
   - BEP printed; backed 100% by silver and gold reserves in the Philippine Treasury and designated US banks.
3. **Emergency / Guerilla Currency (1941–1945):**
   - Local resistance currency issued on various islands (Mindanao, Cebu, Panay, Negros) authorized by General Douglas MacArthur to fund guerilla operations against Japanese forces.
4. **Victory Overprint Notes (Series 1944):**
   - Prepared by the US BEP in Washington, D.C., and shipped with liberation convoys.
   - Distinct bold black overprint VICTORY on the reverse of Philippine Treasury Certificates (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500 Pesos).
   - Central Bank Overprints added in 1949 (out of scope for tonight; 1944 wartime issues strictly in scope).
